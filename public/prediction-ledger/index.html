Iran conflict and negotiations

As of
18 August 2026
Horizon
31 October 2026 · medium/long
Decision unit
Trump and a small U.S. negotiating group; Iranian political leadership and the IRGC; implementation requires multiple states

Question: Which highest-precedence political-military outcome will have occurred by the resolution date? The June memorandum's 60-day negotiating window has expired without a final agreement. Trump says no formal talks are taking place or scheduled while Kushner says backchannel exchanges remain active; Iran says Hormuz will remain closed until its terms are met, and tracked commodity-vessel transit remains far below its prewar level.

47%Prolonged regional attrition
18%Armed armistice, frozen conflict, or reciprocal settlement
8%U.S.-favored coercive settlement
13%Major ground, territorial, or nuclear escalation
9%Iran-favored coercive settlement
5%Regime fracture / loss of central control

Dominant · Ends: preventing an Iranian nuclear capability and reopening Hormuz appear relatively fixed; timing and settlement terms remain negotiable.

Dominant · Feedback: the near-closure of Hormuz and costly rerouting are implemented returned costs, not threatened ones; they make continued conflict expensive without showing that either side will accept the other's terms.

Supporting · Authority: Trump can pause or resume attacks quickly, but Israel, Iran, Gulf governments, armed forces, and inspectors determine durability.

Resolution and update triggers

  • Regime fracture: after 18 August, Iran's central authorities lose effective nationwide command of the regular armed forces or IRGC for at least 14 consecutive days, or a competing authority exercises durable territorial control for that period. Once met, this branch is absorbing. Elite disagreement or protest alone does not qualify.
  • Major escalation: after 18 August, a sustained foreign ground campaign or occupation of populated Iranian territory, Iranian occupation of foreign territory, a confirmed nuclear-device test or use, confirmed weapon assembly, or an attack causing a major radiological release. Once met, this branch is absorbing even if fighting later subsides.
  • U.S.-favored settlement: an agreement operative at the deadline meets the U.S. test and not the Iran test: it provides routine Hormuz passage without Iranian licensing or compulsory fees and adds verified nuclear constraints, with major U.S. coercive measures lifted only conditionally or after compliance.
  • Iran-favored settlement: an agreement operative at the deadline meets the Iran test and not the U.S. test: it recognizes Iranian licensing, routing control, or compulsory fees in Hormuz, or front-loads removal of the blockade, oil sanctions, or asset freezes without comparably verified new nuclear constraints.
  • Armistice, frozen conflict, or reciprocal settlement: an agreement operative at the deadline that meets both or neither coercive-settlement test; or the 30 days immediately before the deadline are strike-free and a stable de facto separation holds. Routine passage means a 30-day average of at least 50% of the prewar 130–140 tracked commodity-vessel transits per day, regular VLCC and LNG transits, and no state-claimed attacks. Passage and an operating negotiation channel distinguish an armistice from a frozen conflict but are not required for the branch.
  • Attrition: all remaining recurring strike or maritime-disruption conditions without a qualifying settlement or armistice.
  • Precedence: regime fracture; major escalation; coercive settlement; armistice, frozen conflict, or reciprocal settlement; attrition. The first two are absorbing events during the forecast window; the settlement and armistice tests must hold at the deadline.
  • Move ten points: a signed verification mechanism, sustained Hormuz reopening, territorial seizure, major U.S. casualties, outside-navy combat, or visible Iranian command fracture.

Greenland governing arrangement

As of
18 August 2026
Horizon
31 December 2026 · medium
Decision unit
U.S., Danish, and Greenlandic political leadership; status changes also require the competent legislatures and, where applicable, Greenlandic voters

Question: As of 23:59 UTC on the resolution date, what is the highest-order publicly verifiable arrangement concerning U.S. defense access or U.S. jurisdiction in Greenland that has been jointly adopted and remains operative? Existing authorities make a site-specific military arrangement easier than a new treaty, while the broader political talks could still produce a visibly trilateral package.

1%Island-wide sovereignty, territorial-status, or free-association change
4%Bounded transfer of sovereignty, territorial title, or exclusive public-law jurisdiction
28%New top-level framework without a sovereignty transfer
31%Site- or capability-specific implementing arrangement within the 1951/2004 framework
36%No qualifying new arrangement, including continued talks or activity under existing authority

Dominant · Authority: the 1951 agreement grants broad U.S. rights inside agreed defense areas, but additional areas require agreement; Greenlandic consent is politically indispensable even where Denmark retains formal defense and treaty authority.

Dominant · Commitment: ownership rhetoric, technical talks, site surveys, and NATO exercises are not equivalent to an adopted legal instrument.

Supporting · Ends: missile warning, Golden Dome integration, North Atlantic surveillance, and denial of adversary footholds can largely be pursued without a sovereignty transfer.

Resolution rules

  • Qualifying instrument: the text is publicly released and operative by the deadline where legally binding. A nonbinding declaration counts only if all three governments adopt it and it names concrete commitments, an implementation body, and an effective date or timetable.
  • Joint adoption: an official instrument issued or signed by the United States and Denmark and signed or formally endorsed by Greenland's government. Leaks, anonymous sourcing, and unilateral characterizations do not qualify.
  • Island-wide change: a legally effective arrangement establishes U.S. sovereignty, U.S. territorial status, or a Compact of Free Association covering Greenland as a whole.
  • Bounded transfer: a legally effective instrument grants the United States sovereign title or exclusive territorial legislative, executive, and adjudicative authority over a defined area. Status-of-forces jurisdiction over U.S. personnel, operational control, leases, and base security under continuing Danish sovereignty do not qualify. The 2004 amendment superseded the original agreement's personnel-jurisdiction clause with the NATO Status of Forces Agreement.
  • New top-level framework: an instrument replaces or generally amends the core 1951/2004 framework, or names implementing bodies and concrete, dated commitments in defense plus at least one of economic or infrastructure commitments, standing trilateral governance, or continuity after a future constitutional-status change. A principles-only communiqué does not qualify.
  • Existing-framework implementation: a public, jointly accepted, site- or capability-specific instrument designates a defense area or materially changes named U.S. rights without qualifying for a higher category.
  • Precedence: island-wide change; bounded transfer; new top-level framework; existing-framework implementation; no qualifying arrangement. A required but incomplete ratification does not count as a legally effective sovereignty transfer.

2004 Igaliku agreement: Greenlandic participation and the current defense area

Greenland implemented U.S. posture

As of
18 August 2026
Horizon
30 June 2027 · medium/long
Decision unit
U.S. Defense and State Departments plus Congress; Denmark holds defense and treaty authority, while Greenland controls important local implementation and its cooperation is politically indispensable

Question: What is the maximum durable increase in U.S. military posture in Greenland, relative to 18 August 2026, that is publicly verified by the resolution date? This forecast separates a negotiated document from operational capacity that actually begins and remains active or recurring at the deadline.

45%No material durable increase beyond the existing Pituffik mission
30%Material expansion at Pituffik only
20%Persistent or recurring U.S. use of one additional named Greenland site
5%Persistent or recurring U.S. use of two or more additional named sites

Dominant · Commitment: a favorable treaty, announced plan, survey, or unperformed contract does not itself create a durable military footprint; physical construction, operational fielding, personnel, and recurring access do.

Dominant · Authority: new-site access requires Danish and Greenlandic cooperation, while durable construction and operations also require U.S. authorization and funding.

Supporting · Feedback: Arctic logistics, environmental and land-use constraints, local benefits, construction seasons, and NATO alternatives determine operational affordability.

Resolution rules

  • Frozen baseline: Pituffik is the sole qualifying durable U.S. site at 23:59 UTC on 18 August 2026. Capabilities, projects, awards, and recurring deployments publicly authorized by then are baseline and do not qualify merely by continuing; a pre-baseline project counts only if it puts a new operational mission capability into service after the baseline.
  • Material expansion at Pituffik: first operational fielding of a new mission capability or permanently based force element, physical construction begun after the baseline that directly enables one, or an actual recurring deployment that has completed at least one cycle by the deadline.
  • Persistent additional-site use: at a named site outside Pituffik, U.S. mission operations or mission-linked physical construction have begun; personnel or equipment have been present for at least 90 consecutive days; or a named U.S. unit or capability has completed one deployment and has a published schedule for a second cycle totaling at least 30 days per year.
  • Site: one distinct physical installation, airfield, or port. Multiple capabilities at the same facility count once.
  • Exclusions: authorizations, budgets, contract awards without physical performance, studies, housing, maintenance, environmental remediation, replacement in kind, one-off exercises, inspections, surveys, port calls, overflights, and NATO activity without a distinct durable U.S. posture increase.
  • Classification: use the highest number of qualifying additional sites. A change in sovereignty is scored in the governing-arrangement forecast and does not alter this operational classification.
  • Move ten points: formal authorization for a named site outside Pituffik, a Greenland-specific military-construction award, a published recurring deployment schedule, cancellation of the three-site plan, or withdrawal of Danish or Greenlandic cooperation.

Post-court tariff regime

As of
2 August 2026
Horizon
20 January 2027 · long
Decision unit
Trump and USTR, constrained by courts, customs administration, markets, and counterparties

Question: How broad will the operative U.S. tariff regime be at the resolution date? The Supreme Court removed one authority, but the administration rebuilt a broad floor under other statutes.

50%Broad floor covers at least 75% of import value; general rate stays below 20%
25%Narrower sector/country regime covers 25–75% of import value
15%Material rollback leaves duties covering less than 25% of import value
10%Expanded regime retains broad coverage with a general rate of 20% or more

Dominant · Ends: the tariff direction appears more fixed than any legal basis, schedule, exemption, or stated justification.

Dominant · Commitment: replacement authorities and continuous collection indicate an implemented program rather than a recurring trial balloon.

Supporting · Feedback: courts, bond and equity markets, consumer prices, foreign concessions, and concentrated lobbying are more likely to change exemptions than direction.

Resolution and update triggers

  • Classification: use the latest authoritative estimate of import value subject to additional Trump-era duties. The 20%-or-more general-rate branch takes precedence over the other broad-coverage branch.
  • Move ten points: adverse final court rulings on Section 301 implementation, a large Treasury-market dislocation, sustained inflation acceleration attributed to tariffs, congressional restriction, or a negotiated multi-country rollback.
  • Patronage test: exemptions count as particularistic only when access or unequal treatment is documented, not merely because a sector benefits.

Administrative-state restructuring

As of
2 August 2026
Horizon
20 January 2028 · horizon
Decision unit
White House and OPM; agencies, courts, unions, Congress, and labor markets determine implementation

Question: What condition best describes the Schedule Policy/Career program and presidential control of independent agencies at the resolution date?

35%Durable centralization with limited severe service failure
30%Hybrid adaptation: authority survives but implementation remains uneven
20%Centralization accompanied by significant multi-agency capacity degradation
10%Substantial judicial or congressional rollback
5%Largely symbolic implementation with little durable output change

Dominant · Authority: this directly tests whether personal supervision can be embedded in law, staffing, review, and repeatable organizational routines.

Dominant · Commitment: eligibility and executive orders matter less than completed transfers, removals, replacements, changed decisions, and persistence.

Supporting · Feedback: litigation, vacancies, recruitment, backlogs, technical failures, and coalition costs determine whether centralization remains politically affordable.

Resolution and update triggers

  • Durable centralization: a majority of the roughly 8,000 identified positions are transferred or otherwise subjected to the new removal regime, and independent-agency supervision remains operational after major litigation.
  • Capacity degradation: sustained vacancies, backlogs, missed statutory duties, or technical failures are documented across at least three major agencies and linked to staffing or politicization.
  • Classification: rollback takes precedence if the core authorities no longer operate; otherwise use implementation breadth and the capacity test, with significant degradation taking precedence over limited-failure centralization.
  • Move ten points: final Supreme Court rulings, appropriations restrictions, large-scale removals, agency-level implementation data, or repeated service failures.

Question: How will the federal response be publicly characterized by the resolution date? The observed outcome was the first branch: no public high-confidence attribution of this campaign to Iran and no response beyond technical containment or limited measures.

Resolution: by the deadline, no qualifying federal attribution, charging document, sanctions finding, or acknowledged retaliatory response tied to the 27 July MicroLogix incident set was identified in the specified public sources.

Classification discipline: federal agencies had separately attributed an earlier, technically broader PLC campaign to Iranian-affiliated actors. Their 30 July notices did not publicly establish that the later MicroLogix incident set had the same operator.

Result: the forecast succeeded on its stated public-characterization question. This does not establish that Iran was uninvolved or prevent a later attribution.

Resolution rules retained from the forecast

  • Campaign scope: the PLC incidents described in the FBI and EPA's 30 July alert, beginning 27 July; evidence about a separate campaign does not transfer automatically.
  • High-confidence affiliation: a named federal agency or coordinated federal statement attributes this incident set to Iran, the IRGC, or actors acting on their direction, or a charging document or sanctions finding makes the link.
  • Limited measures: alerts, investigation, defensive assistance, technical containment, charges, or targeted sanctions without an acknowledged offensive-cyber or military retaliation.
  • Escalation: the president or a named federal agency acknowledges retaliatory offensive-cyber or military action, or another response beyond the limited measures above, tied to this incident set.
  • Redirection: a named federal agency or comparably authoritative technical finding identifies a non-Iranian state, independent criminal group, or non-state operator as more likely responsible.
  • Precedence: escalation; attribution redirected away from Iran; Iranian affiliation with no more than limited measures; no qualifying attribution with containment or limited measures.
  • Cutoff: the public forecast named 16 August but did not specify a timezone. This closeout adopts 23:59 UTC retrospectively for reproducibility; the ambiguity did not affect classification because no qualifying item appeared under plausible end-of-day cutoffs.
  • Negative-search protocol: through the cutoff, the review covered FBI cyber alerts, CISA advisories, Justice Department press releases, Treasury/OFAC actions, and White House or named-agency announcements. The result is limited to those public sources.
